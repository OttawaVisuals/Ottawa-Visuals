Wealth

The tax Canada doesn't have

Canada has had no tax on inherited estates since 1972. the federal government replaced its estate tax with a capital-gains tax triggered at death, and the provinces' succession duties were gone by 1985. How Canada taxes wealth instead: milestones

  • 1972Federal estate & gift tax repealed. Capital gains taxed for the first time, at a 50% inclusion rate, with deemed disposition at death.
  • 1988–90Inclusion rate raised to 66⅔%, then 75%.
  • 2000Cut back to 66⅔% (Feb.), then 50% (Oct.), where it still stands.
  • 2021The budget officer (PBO) costs a 1% annual tax on family wealth above $10M at $10.9B in its first year, after assuming 35% of wealth escapes through avoidance. Not adopted.
  • 2024–25A proposed 66⅔% inclusion rate on gains above $250k is announced in Budget 2024, then cancelled in March 2025.

Read before quoting

What correlation can and can't tell you here

One country, one century, a handful of regimes. The high-rate era (1940s–70s) was also the era of postwar reconstruction, the baby boom, strong unions, cheap energy and catch-up productivity growth, in most rich countries at once. Annual data points aren't independent: 100 years of data contain maybe five or six real policy regimes. Correlations below are descriptive, not causal.

Why the top-share link is more credible than the growth link. There's a direct mechanism: a high top rate removes most of the reward for bargaining for, or booking, very high income, and reduces how much income compounds into wealth. Saez & Veall (2005) found Canada's top shares moved with US shares even when Canadian rates didn't, so part of the post-1980 rise is a North American labour-market story (executive pay, finance) rather than Canadian tax policy alone.

Donations. The donation tax credit (75% on the first $400, falling to 33⅓%, max $650) is non-refundable: a filer who owes no income tax gets no rebate. That adds a direct tax link to the income gradient.
