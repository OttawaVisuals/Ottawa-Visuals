The wealthiest 1% of families

The Parliamentary Budget Officer estimates that the top 1% of economic families held 24% of Canada’s net wealth in 2023. Its model supplements StatCan survey data with billionaire lists to better capture the very wealthy.

This is a separate estimate with significant uncertainty. Its family unit, method and reference year differ from the household charts above.

Sources, method & limitations

What is measured?

Net worth = total assets − total liabilities. It includes real estate, financial assets, life insurance and pensions, and other non-financial assets, less mortgages and other debts. Wealth is a stock at a point in time; income is a flow over a period. These charts rank households by wealth.

Main source

StatCan Table 36-10-0660-01, Distributions of Household Economic Accounts (DHEA). These estimates combine national accounts with survey and other inputs; they are not a fresh survey of every household each quarter.

  • Selection: Canada; net worth; five wealth quintiles; Q4 in each year from 2010 to 2025. Earlier years only have Q4 observations. No missing quarters are filled or interpolated.
  • Shares: each quintile’s published “Distribution of value.” Combined bottom-40% and middle-40% shares are calculated from the groups’ total dollar values divided by the all-households total, then rounded to one decimal. Published rounding can make shares sum slightly above or below 100%.
  • Gap: top-20% share minus bottom-40% share, in percentage points. The compared groups have different population sizes; this is StatCan’s wealth-gap measure.
  • Average dollars: the published “Value per household,” in nominal dollars. They are not inflation-adjusted, medians, or percentile cutoffs.
  • Changing membership: a wealth group can contain different households each year. These are distribution snapshots, not the fortunes of a fixed set of families.
  • Negative wealth: a group can owe more than it owns. Negative shares are retained, including in the chart scale.

Coverage and uncertainty

The Survey of Financial Security, an input to these estimates, covers the ten provinces and excludes Indigenous communities and collective dwellings. It underrepresents very wealthy families. DHEA also relies on modelling assumptions and prior-period distributions for some components. Asset valuations and future source revisions can change the estimates. These figures describe Canada; they do not establish Ottawa’s wealth distribution.
