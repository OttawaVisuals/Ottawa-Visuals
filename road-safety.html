The Math

Simplified model — treat with caution. These calculations assume a perfectly inelastic collision between two rigid bodies: all momentum transfers instantaneously at the moment of impact. Real collisions deviate substantially. Crumple zones deform over ~100–200 ms, absorbing a large fraction of the vehicle's kinetic energy as heat and structural deformation before it reaches the pedestrian — the peak force is spread over time, not delivered all at once. The human body isn't rigid either: flesh, fat, joints, and organs deform and redirect force, further breaking the rigid-body assumption. Contact geometry also matters — a sedan's hood may catch and partially decelerate the pedestrian; a pickup's high bumper transfers force to the chest rather than the legs; both produce trajectories nothing like the 45° ballistic arc shown below. In practice, pedestrians wrap around hoods or are pushed forward and slide along the ground.

Methodology & sources
Kinetic energy
KE = ½ × vehicle mass × impact speed². Energy carried by the vehicle at the moment of impact.
Momentum transfer
Launch speed = (vehicle mass × vehicle speed) ÷ (vehicle mass + pedestrian mass). Assumes instantaneous, perfectly inelastic collision between rigid bodies.
Launch trajectory
Range = v² ÷ g, max height = range ÷ 4, for a 45° launch with no air resistance (g = 9.81 m/s²). A theoretical ceiling, not a real prediction.
Injury risk
Risk curves from IIHS (Monfort & Mueller, 2024/2025), giving moderate/serious/fatal injury probability by impact speed for median-height car and pickup. Compact/sedan use the car curve; pickup and full-size use the pickup curve; SUV/crossover uses the average of the two. Source data covers pedestrians aged 16+.
Vehicle masses
Approximate curb weights for common categories in the Canadian fleet (used for KE and momentum calculations only, not injury risk curves).
