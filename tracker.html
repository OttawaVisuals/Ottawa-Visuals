🚦 Ottawa RTO Watch

🔨 Building · ~35% open the page ↗

In one sentence: a before/after look at how return-to-office mandates have changed Ottawa commutes and transit reliability — fed by two independent collectors that log data no public source keeps history for.

Where the data comes from

TomTom Routing / Traffic Flow / Incidents APIspolled every 5–60 min from a Raspberry Pi
OC Transpo GTFS-Realtime feedTripUpdates + VehiclePositions, polled the same way
OC Transpo KPI spreadsheets (Open Ottawa)official service & ridership KPIs, snapshotted weekly — rolling ~13-month windows

How it was built

1 · PollPi cron hits TomTom + GTFS-RT on a peak/off-peak gated schedule (Traffic/scripts/poll_traffic.py, OC_Transpo/scripts/poll_gtfsrt.py)
→
2 · AppendEach sample appends a row to committed CSVs (Traffic/data/, OC_Transpo/rt_data/)
→
3 · SnapshotWeekly job archives OC Transpo's official rolling-window KPI spreadsheets before they age out
→
4 · rto.htmlReads the accumulating CSVs — a real time series builds up week over week
Full methodology, in plain language

Why this exists — TomTom and OC Transpo don't keep history

TomTom's live traffic-index page for Ottawa shows current speeds and jams but no history. OC Transpo's public GTFS-RT feed reports live vehicle positions but archives nothing. Both collectors exist purely to build a historical series the public sources don't offer.

Traffic — sampled proxy, not the whole network

Rather than the whole road network, the collector samples 5 commute corridors, 15 road segments, and 1 incident bounding box (defined in Traffic/corridors.json), then derives the same headline numbers TomTom's live panel shows (average speed, congestion %, jam count, jam length, rush-hour extra time) from those samples. Cadence is gated in the poller itself (weekday rush hours 06:30–09:30 / 15:30–18:30 every ~15 min, otherwise hourly) so it follows Ottawa DST correctly regardless of when GitHub's cron actually fires.

Transit — the feed is bus-only, with no delay fields

Verified live on 2026-07-11: OC Transpo's beta GTFS-RT feed sets no delay fields at all — TripUpdates instead carry absolute predicted arrival times per stop, and no route 1/2/4 (O-Train) entities were ever observed. So the live CSV only directly gives cancellations and fleet activity; true on-time performance has to be computed offline by comparing the raw predicted-arrival archive against the static GTFS schedule — which is why the raw archive (kept outside the repo, ~15 MB/day gzipped) is effectively required, not optional. O-Train reliability comes from the official KPI spreadsheets instead, since GTFS-RT never sees trains.

The rolling-window trap

OC Transpo's own KPI spreadsheets on Open Ottawa are rolling ~13-month windows — older months silently disappear as new ones are published. The weekly snapshotter hashes each file's content and only saves a new dated copy when something actually changed, so a cron job can run weekly forever without duplicating unchanged files, while still preserving months that would otherwise be lost when the window rolls forward.

    Other options: TomTom's Area Analytics product covers the whole network but only as a paid 30-day trial with no ongoing free tier — usable for a one-time historical backfill (see PROJECTS.md), not a permanent forward-looking feed.

    Traffic/README.md §"What it collects".

    Confirmed by inspecting the live feed directly, not assumed. The live CSV tracks cancellations and fleet activity only; on-time % has to be derived later from the raw archive.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 15 min

    OC_Transpo/README.md §"GTFS-RT collector (Raspberry Pi)".
  • source limitOC Transpo's own KPI spreadsheets are rolling ~13-month windows.

🗳️ Ottawa Ward Elections

✅ Live · ~90% open the tool ↗

In one sentence: ward-by-ward turnout and vote shares for mayor and council, 2010–2022, mapped onto the current 24-ward boundaries even though those boundaries changed in 2022.

Where the data comes from

Elections Ottawa — ward resultsofficial per-ward results, each municipal election cycle
City of Ottawa — 2022 ward boundariesGeoJSON used directly by the page

How it was built

1 · CollectPer-ward, per-year results into ward_results.csv
→
2 · Reconcile boundariesMap every pre-2022 ward onto its 2022 successor (BuildWardData.py)
→
3 · One web pageFetches the boundary GeoJSON straight from GitHub's raw URL — no local build step to view it
Full methodology, in plain language

The core problem: Ottawa redrew its wards in 2022

One ward was added (24 total, up from 23), which means some 2022 wards don't correspond to any single historical ward. BuildWardData.py classifies every 2022 ward into one of five change types — direct, renamed, minor (small boundary tweak), split (territory moved to another ward), or new (assembled from pieces of others) — and attaches a caveat to any ward whose historical numbers don't cleanly represent its current area.

The one estimated ward: Barrhaven East (Ward 24)

Ward 24 didn't exist before 2022 — it was carved roughly 60% from the old Barrhaven ward (Ward 3) and 40% from old Gloucester-South Nepean (Ward 22). Its 2010–2018 history is not real election data: it's a weighted average of those two source wards' participation rates, using registered-voter counts as weights. The page/data explicitly labels this as an estimate, not an actual result for that territory.

Wards 3 and 22 also carry caveats

Because part of old Ward 3 (Barrhaven) became Ward 24 and part of old Ward 22 lost territory to both Ward 3 and Ward 24, their pre-2022 historical figures reflect a larger area than the current ward — a straightforward "boundary shrank" caveat, no estimation involved.

⚠️ Assumptions & judgment calls — check these

  • judgment callWard 24's 2010–2018 history is an estimate: a 60/40 weighted average of two old wards, not real results.

    The 60/40 split is an approximation of the 2022 population/territory split, weighted by each source ward's registered-voter count per year.

    Other options: show no history at all for Ward 24 (loses a trend line entirely), or a straight unweighted 50/50 average (ignores that the two source wards have different sizes).

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 2 min
    1. Open Ward 24 on the map and check the caveat text — it states plainly that pre-2022 figures are an estimate, not an actual result for that specific territory.
    Ottawa_Elections/BuildWardData.py, NEW_CAVEAT dict; surfaced as caveat in ward_data.json.
  • source limitWards 3 and 22's historical figures reflect a larger area than their current 2022 boundary.

    No estimation is involved here — the raw historical numbers are simply for the old, bigger ward.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 2 min
    1. Open Ward 3 (Barrhaven) or Ward 22 (Riverside South–Findlay Creek) and read the split caveat — it names exactly which neighbourhoods moved out.
    Ottawa_Elections/BuildWardData.py, SPLIT_CAVEAT dict.

🏛️ Ontario Elections (coalition scenarios)

🔨 Building · ~70% open the tool ↗

In one sentence: province-wide turnout since 1981, the 2025 general election riding by riding, and a "what if the opposition had consolidated" model that reallocates opposition votes to whoever led each riding.

Where the data comes from

Elections Ontario — Explorer export2025-02-27 general election, candidate + riding + province level
ELECTORAL_DISTRICT shapefileriding boundaries, hand-cut into 3 zoom-level GeoJSONs

How it was built

1 · Aggregate votesExplorer CSV → data.js (build_data.py)
→
2 · Wrap boundariesOntario/Ottawa/Southern-Ontario GeoJSONs → geo.js, untouched
→
3 · Turnout history1981–2025 province turnout + winner, plus per-riding turnout for the last 3 elections → turnout.js
→
4 · One web pageONelections.html — map + consolidation model
Full methodology, in plain language

Why the map looks like clean rectangles

The three boundary GeoJSONs were hand-cut in mapshaper directly in the source shapefile's own projected coordinate system (Ontario Lambert Conformal Conic, in metres) — not reprojected to latitude/longitude. That projection is exactly what gives many ridings their clean rectangular look; reprojecting would add a few degrees of grid-convergence rotation and skew that shape. The build script deliberately renders the planar coordinates as-is, as a flat Cartesian plane, rather than "fixing" them into a geographic projection.

Matching province-wide winners back to 1981

Candidate-level riding data only exists for the years in the Explorer export, but Elections Ontario's separate party-level seat-count export goes back to 1981. A small keyword matcher (match_party_code) maps each year's top-seat party name to a party code, so the turnout chart can show every election's governing party back to 1981 even without riding-level detail for the older years.

The consolidation model's built-in simplification

The "what if" model reallocates Liberal/NDP/Green votes in each riding to whichever of those parties already led there, holding total turnout fixed. It does not model actual voter behaviour (some opposition voters might not have voted for the merged option, or might have voted PC instead) — it answers a narrower, mechanical question: how many ridings would have flipped on the existing vote totals alone if the opposition vote had been cast as one bloc.

⚠️ Assumptions & judgment calls — check these

  • judgment callThe consolidation model reallocates opposition votes mechanically, holding turnout fixed.

    It's a "same votes, one ballot line" counterfactual, not a behavioural prediction of how people would have actually voted under a merged party.

    Other options: a swing model that assumes some fraction of consolidated voters would defect to the PCs (adds a second, harder-to-justify assumption); or no what-if model at all (simpler, but loses the "how close was it really" framing).

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 2 min
    1. Pick a riding the model flips and check the page's own explanation of the mechanic — it should describe exactly this "reallocate to the leader" rule, not a polling-based swing.
    Ontario_Elections/ONelections.html "what if" panel; Ontario_Elections/build_data.py header comment.
  • judgment callThe map uses the source shapefile's own projected coordinates, unreprojected.

    Gives the map its distinctive clean rectangular riding shapes; the alternative (true lat/lon) would look more "normal" geographically but less crisp.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 2 min
    1. Compare a riding's shape on the tool against the same riding on a standard lat/lon map (e.g. Google Maps) — the rotation difference is the tell.
    Ontario_Elections/build_data.py, comment above geo_sources.
  • judgment callPre-2025 province-wide winners are matched from party name text, not a maintained ID table.

    A small keyword matcher (substring checks like "progressive conservative", "liberal") turns each year's top-seat party name into a code — good enough since party names have been stable, but it would silently miss a genuinely new party name.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 2 min
    1. Check the turnout chart's winner colour/label against Wikipedia's list of Ontario general elections for a couple of older years (e.g. 1990, 1995) — they should match.
    Ontario_Elections/build_data.py, match_party_code.

🏡 Mortgage / Housing Affordability Calculator

✅ Live · ~90% open the tool ↗

In one sentence: enter income, down payment and rate to see your CMHC/OSFI stress-tested maximum mortgage, monthly costs, and where that lands against two decades of Ottawa benchmark prices.

How it was built

→
2 · Build JSONPipeline/build_json.py compacts everything into the JSON/ files the page reads
→
3 · One web pagemortgage.html — no server, no live API calls
Full methodology, in plain language

The qualifying rule, applied exactly as a lender would

Qualifying uses OSFI's B-20 guideline: Gross Debt Service (GDS) ≤ 39% and Total Debt Service (TDS) ≤ 44%, tested at the greater of your contract rate + 2%, or the Bank of Canada's 5.25% qualifying floor — the same stress test a federally-regulated lender applies, not a simplified approximation.

Why CREA benchmark, not raw MLS average price

CREA's Home Price Index is a quality-adjusted "typical home" measure, deliberately chosen over a simple average/median sale price because it isn't skewed month-to-month by an unusual mix of what happened to sell (e.g. a run of high-end sales).

Filling the gaps between FSA, province and national data

Income and spending data exist at different granularities — census income is available by FSA, but Statistics Canada's household spending survey is only published at the province/national level. Where a per-FSA figure doesn't exist, the calculator falls back to the province-level figure, and from there to the national figure — so every FSA always resolves to some number, at a clearly coarser level of detail than the postal-code-specific ones.

⚠️ Assumptions & judgment calls — check these

  • standard factorStress test: GDS ≤ 39%, TDS ≤ 44%, at rate + 2% or 5.25%, whichever is higher.

    This is OSFI's own published B-20 rule, not a house assumption.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 15 min
    1. Compare the calculator's qualifying math against OSFI's B-20 guideline text (or CMHC's public explainer) for the GDS/TDS ceilings and the qualifying-rate rule.
    index.html REPORTS[] "mortgage" §method; Mortgage/Pipeline/build_json.py.
  • judgment callOttawa home prices use CREA's benchmark (HPI), not a raw average/median sale price.

    Other options: average sale price (simpler, but swings with the mix of homes that happened to sell that month) or median sale price (more robust than average, but still mix-sensitive). HPI is CREA's own quality-adjusted alternative.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 15 min
    1. Compare a recent month's benchmark price in the tool against CREA's own published MLS HPI figure for Ottawa — they should match.
    Mortgage/README.md §"Method"; Mortgage/Static/crea_benchmarks_latest.csv.
  • judgment callIncome/spending data falls back FSA → province → national when a finer figure doesn't exist.

    Keeps the calculator working for every postal code, at the cost of some FSAs showing a coarser (provincial or national) figure without it being obvious at a glance.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 2 min
    1. Enter a smaller/rural Ottawa-area FSA and a downtown one — compare whether the household-spending figure shown actually differs between them (it won't, if both fell back to the same provincial number).

🌡️ Ottawa Weather / Climate Evolution

✅ Live · ~90% open the tool ↗

In one sentence: 135 years of daily + 73 years of hourly Environment Canada records, showing not just "warmer" but which kind of extreme has actually changed.

Where the data comes from

How it was built

→

→

→
5 · One web pageweather.html reads 5 committed JSON files
Full methodology, in plain language

Why 30-year normals, not first-vs-last-decade deltas

Daytime-max heat (days ≥ 30 °C) is noisy and roughly flat across 30-year windows — the 1910s already ran about as hot as today — so comparing single decades would show a "hot days" trend that's really a window artifact. The climate-shift block instead compares two 30-year normals (1891–1920 vs 1996–2025). The robust signals that actually hold up are overnight warmth (warm nights roughly tripled), winter mean (+3 °C), a collapsing extreme-cold tail (down about two-thirds), longer seasons, and a snow→rain shift — which is why the dashboard's hero KPI is "warm nights", deliberately not "hot days".

Two record ranges that don't overlap, labelled honestly

All-time temperature/precipitation records use the daily series (1889+), so "hottest day ever" reflects the full history — Ottawa's record highs predate 1953. Humidex, wind chill, wind speed and any hour-resolution leaderboard can only come from the hourly series (1953+); the dashboard labels those "1953+" so the two ranges don't read as contradictory.

Lightning is treated as a weaker, supplementary source

The volunteer detector network backing the lightning data has grown over time — day-to-day availability was patchy in early 2021 — so a rising strike count over the dataset's life may partly reflect more detectors coming online, not more actual lightning. Unlike the calibrated ECCC series, it's explicitly presented as colour, not a trend claim.

A quietly-checked gap

Hourly precipitation amount is essentially never populated for this station — checked across every decade, both stations, before deciding not to compute an hourly rainfall-intensity index from it. Precipitation totals instead come entirely from the daily pipeline.

⚠️ Assumptions & judgment calls — check these

  • judgment callThe climate-shift KPIs compare two 30-year normals, not first-vs-last decade.

    Other options: a simple first-decade-vs-last-decade delta (what many casual comparisons use) — rejected because it's noisy for daytime heat and can show a trend that's really a decade-endpoint artifact.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 15 min

  • source limitBoth series are spliced from two ECCC stations, filling gaps day-by-day rather than a hard cutover.

    Ottawa's official record isn't one continuous station — it moved over the decades. The splice is a standard practice for long Canadian climate series, not unique to this project.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 15 min
    1. Look up ECCC station 4333 vs 49568 (or 4337 vs 49568 for hourly) on climate.weather.gc.ca and confirm their active date ranges match the splice points described in Weather/README.md.
    Weather/README.md §"Daily" and §"Hourly".
  • source limitLightning counts may rise partly because the volunteer sensor network grew, not just because of more real lightning.

    Explicitly flagged as the weakest source in the dashboard — supplementary colour, not a calibrated trend.

    ✅ Verify it yourself ⏱️ 2 min

🏛️ City Hall eScribe Indexer

🔨 Pipeline built · data only, no page yet

In one sentence: a structured, queryable CSV index of what every City of Ottawa committee/council/commission discussed and decided — meetings, agenda items, motions, votes and attachments — straight from the public eScribe portal.

Where the data comes from

City of Ottawa eScribe portalcalendar API back to ~2019, agenda/minutes pages per meeting

How it was built

1 · Enumerate meetingseScribe's calendar API, filterable by committee & year range
→
2 · Parse the richest pagePostMinutes → Minutes → Agenda fallback chain, per meeting
→
3 · Five joinable CSVsmeetings / agenda_items / motions / votes / attachments, joined on meeting_id
Full methodology, in plain language

Nested agenda items are handled deliberately

Agenda items can nest (e.g. 6 → 6.1); the parser is built so a parent item doesn't double-count its children's motions, votes or attachments when rolled up.

Politeness and resumability

This hits a public-records site with a real browser User-Agent (the portal blocks default bot user-agents) and the OS-native trust store for TLS. Every meeting is checkpointed, so a run interrupted overnight resumes exactly where it left off rather than restarting.

⚠️ Known limitations — check these

  • source limitThe calendar API only reaches back to ~2019 — older meetings likely live in a separate archive.
    CityHall_Index/README.md §"Notes & limits".
  • judgment callRecorded votes are captured as one name-string per tally, not exploded to one row per councillor.

    Good enough for "how did the vote go", but a clean "how did my councillor vote" dataset needs a further parsing pass on that string.

    Other options: explode to per-councillor rows immediately — held off until the name-string format is validated across more committees, to avoid silently mis-splitting names.
